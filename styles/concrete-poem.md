# Concrete Poem

> Typographic art: words arranged as shapes, sentences that draw. Ink on paper, where the poem's layout is the poem — a circle of nouns, a staircase of verbs, silence used as punctuation.

## Type

- **Display:** Major Mono Display — monospace as architecture: every glyph the same width, so words stack into exact shapes. Headlines are built, not typeset.
- **Body:** Space Mono — the voice of the poem itself. Line breaks are composition; leading and alignment carry meaning.
- **Mono:** Space Mono — everything here is mono; the distinction is size and weight, not family. Captions in small regular, poems in larger regular, rubrics in bold accent red.

Google Fonts: `Major+Mono+Display`, `Space+Mono:ital,wght@0,400;0,700;1,400`

## Spacing & shape

- The grid is the canvas: generous paper margins, compositions centered or deliberately off-center like a printed broadside.
- Whitespace is active material — a half-page of nothing is a loud decision.
- Shapes are built from text blocks: circles, waves, columns, staircases. Borders, if any, are hairline plate marks.
- One composition per viewport; concrete poetry does not stack into a feed.

## Motion

Slow and deliberate: words fade in sequence to reveal the shape being drawn, letter by letter, like watching someone write. Scroll can rotate or reassemble a composition — but gently. Motion should feel like the poem being set in type, not animated for effect.

## Do

- Make at least one section a real shape-poem: words arranged into a circle, a wave, a column — the shape must relate to the subject.
- Let alignment do the work: a staircase of lines needs no decoration at all.
- Use the rubric red for one word per composition, like a rubricated manuscript initial.
- Caption everything like a gallery plate: title, medium, year, in small mono under each piece.

## Don't

- Don't decorate with clip art or illustrations — the words are the image; adding pictures breaks the contract.
- Don't center everything by default — concrete poetry lives in deliberate, meaningful asymmetry.
- Don't use a second type family for contrast — the discipline of one monospace voice is the whole point.
- Don't rush the reveal — if the shape builds too fast, the reader sees animation instead of poetry.

## The one weird thing

Build the hero as an actual concrete poem for the subject: the product or event name arranged in a relevant shape — a wine glass for a tasting, a wave for a festival, a doorway for a gallery. Or add a "typesetter mode": hovering a composition highlights each line's word count in tiny mono, revealing the construction like printer's marks. Pick one.

Poets, galleries, literary festivals, book launches, typography events.

## Lineage

The concrete-poetry principle that typography is a visual art before it is a reading aid — form carries meaning; the broadside tradition of the poem as a printed object to be looked at; the rubrication habit of marking the essential word in red.
